    What building it proved the spec had wrong

    Five analyze passes hardened the spec before a line was written, and caught two serious holes — a refused message in crisis got no helpline, and the one disclosure Principle V names outright had no mechanism. Both were fixed on paper. Then running the code against the real toolkit falsified three more, none of which review had found:

    1. Sheet ids are not all unnatural strings. The research said the slugs "never appear in a legitimate answer". That is false for 16 of the 75 — numerology, death, luck, property, relationship. The product's own refusal copy says "numerology", so every refusal was being withheld and replaced by the safe message. Caught on the first run, by an invariant added during an analyze pass: the fixed copy must survive its own scan.
    2. The stopword list was an order of magnitude too small. The toolkit's 2,001 matters yield 1,544 single English words, and 6 of 15 plainly off-topic messages were admitted by generic words the toolkit happens to use — script, change, buy, news, draft. 546 reviewed stopwords later, three remain: buy, election and job are real subject matter in another sense, and stopwording "job" would refuse the corpus's own "will I get a government job?". That is the design working as intended — and it exposed a missing instrument: nothing counts an off-topic message that was allowed.
    3. The crisis rule fired on an ordinary question. "When will I die?" satisfied "first-person subject + modal + harm word" — and it is one of the commonest astrology questions there is. Showing a crisis line there would train users to ignore it: exactly the harm the research rejected keying on harm words to avoid. The rule now reads an opening interrogative as a question about the future, unless the phrase is reflexive — "should I kill myself" is still intent.
